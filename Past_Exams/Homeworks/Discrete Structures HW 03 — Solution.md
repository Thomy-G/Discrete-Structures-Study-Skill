## 📌 Solution 1: Cardinality of $101$-Free Infinite Binary Sequences

**Problem:** Calculate $|A|$ where $A = \{ f \in \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N} \mid 101 \text{ does not appear as a consecutive substring} \}$.

### Formal Proof:
1. **Upper Bound:**
   By definition, $A$ is a subset of all infinite binary sequences:
   $$A \subseteq \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$$
   Therefore:
   $$|A| \le |\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}| = 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph$$

2. **Lower Bound:**
   We construct an explicit injection from the full set of binary sequences $\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$ into $A$.
   Define the alphabet substitution mapping $\sigma: \{0, 1\} \to \{0, 1\}^2$:
   $$\sigma(0) = 00, \quad \sigma(1) = 11$$
   For any infinite binary sequence $s = (s_1, s_2, s_3, \dots) \in \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$, define the sequence:
   $$\Phi(s) = \sigma(s_1) \sigma(s_2) \sigma(s_3) \dots \in \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$$
   - **Verification that $\Phi(s) \in A$:**
     In any sequence generated by $\Phi$, all $0$s appear in runs of even length $\ge 2$, and all $1$s appear in runs of even length $\ge 2$.
     The substring $101$ requires a single isolated $0$ flanked by $1$s. But in $\Phi(s)$, any occurrence of $0$ is part of a block $00$. Thus, $101$ **cannot occur** in $\Phi(s)$.
     Therefore, $\Phi(s) \in A$ for all $s \in \{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$.
   - **Injectivity:**
     If $s \ne t$, they differ at some first index $i$. Then their $i$-th two-bit blocks $\sigma(s_i) \ne \sigma(t_i)$ differ, so $\Phi(s) \ne \Phi(t)$.
     Thus $\Phi$ is injective, which implies:
     $$|A| \ge |\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}| = 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph$$

3. **Conclusion:**
   By the **Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein Theorem**, $|A| \le \aleph$ and $|A| \ge \aleph$ imply:
   $$|A| = \aleph = 2^{\aleph_0}$$
   $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 2: Leaves in a Tree with Maximum Degree $\Delta$

**Problem:** Let $T = (V, E)$ be a tree with maximum degree $\Delta(T) = \Delta$. Prove that $T$ has at least $\Delta$ leaves.

### Formal Proof:
1. Let $r \in V$ be a vertex achieving the maximum degree: $\deg(r) = \Delta$.
2. If $\Delta = 1$, $T = K_2$, which has $2 \ge 1$ leaves.
3. For $\Delta \ge 2$:
   - Root the tree at $r$.
   - The root $r$ has $\Delta$ distinct neighbors: $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_\Delta$.
   - Removing $r$ from $T$ splits the acyclic graph into exactly $\Delta$ connected components: $T_1, T_2, \dots, T_\Delta$, where $v_i \in V(T_i)$.
4. Each component $T_i$ is connected and acyclic, hence a **tree**.
5. In each subtree $T_i$:
   - If $|V(T_i)| = 1$, then $V(T_i) = \{v_i\}$. In the original tree $T$, the only neighbor of $v_i$ was $r$, so $\deg_T(v_i) = 1$. Thus $v_i$ is a leaf of $T$.
   - If $|V(T_i)| \ge 2$, every finite tree with at least 2 vertices has at least two leaves. At most one of those leaves can be $v_i$ (since $v_i$ was connected to $r$). The second leaf $w_i \in V(T_i)$ ($w_i \ne v_i$) had no edge to $r$, so $\deg_T(w_i) = \deg_{T_i}(w_i) = 1$. Thus $w_i$ is a leaf in $T$.
6. Since each of the $\Delta$ components contains at least one leaf of $T$, and the components are pairwise disjoint, $T$ contains at least $\Delta$ distinct leaves. $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 3: Component Parity in Odd-Degree Regular Graphs

**Problem:** Let $G = (V, E)$ be a $k$-regular disconnected graph. Prove that if $k$ is odd, every connected component of $G$ has an even number of vertices.

### Formal Proof:
1. Let $C \subseteq V$ be any connected component of $G$.
2. Since $G$ is disconnected, there are no edges between $C$ and $V \setminus C$.
3. Therefore, for every vertex $v \in C$, all of its neighbors lie entirely inside $C$:
   $$\deg_{G[C]}(v) = \deg_G(v) = k$$
4. Thus, the induced subgraph $G[C]$ is a $k$-regular graph on $n_C = |C|$ vertices.
5. By the **Handshaking Lemma** applied to $G[C]$:
   $$\sum_{v \in C} \deg_{G[C]}(v) = 2 |E(G[C])|$$
6. Since every vertex has degree $k$:
   $$k \cdot n_C = 2 |E(G[C])|$$
7. The right-hand side $2 |E(G[C])|$ is an even integer. Therefore, $k \cdot n_C$ must be an even integer.
8. We are given that $k$ is an **odd** integer. The product of two integers is even if and only if at least one of them is even. Since $k$ is odd, $n_C = |C|$ must be **even**.
   $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 4: True / False Graph Theory Statements

### Part (a): 2-Regular Graph with Even Vertices is Bipartite (FALSE)
- **Disproof by Counterexample:**
  Let $G = C_3 \cup C_3$ (the disjoint union of two 3-cycles / triangles).
  - $G$ has $|V| = 3 + 3 = 6$ vertices, which is **even**.
  - Every vertex in each triangle has degree $2$, so $G$ is **2-regular**.
  - However, $G$ contains odd cycles ($C_3$).
  - By König's Bipartite Theorem, a graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no odd cycles.
  - Since $G$ contains odd cycles, $G$ is **not bipartite**. $\blacksquare$

---

### Part (b): High-Degree Vertex $\deg(v) \ge \frac{3}{4}|V|$ in Connected Bipartite Graph (TRUE)
- **Proof by Construction:**
  Consider the star graph $K_{1, n-1}$ on $n$ vertices with bipartition $A = \{u\}$ and $B = \{v_1, \dots, v_{n-1}\}$.
  - $K_{1, n-1}$ is bipartite (complete bipartite) and connected.
  - The hub vertex $u$ has degree $\deg(u) = n - 1$.
  - We require $\deg(u) \ge \frac{3}{4}n$, which translates to:
    $$n - 1 \ge \frac{3}{4}n \iff \frac{1}{4}n \ge 1 \iff n \ge 4$$
  - For $|V| = 6 > 4$, the star graph $K_{1, 5}$ has $\deg(u) = 5$.
    Check: $\frac{3}{4} |V| = \frac{3}{4} \cdot 6 = 4.5$.
    Indeed, $5 \ge 4.5$.
  Thus such a graph exists. $\blacksquare$

---

### Part (c): Graph with $n \ge 3$ and $m \ge n$ Contains a Cycle (TRUE)
- **Proof:**
  Assume for contradiction that $G$ contains **no cycles**.
  Then $G$ is a **forest** consisting of $k \ge 1$ connected components $T_1, \dots, T_k$.
  By Solution 2 (Forest Edge Invariant), the number of edges in any forest on $n$ vertices with $k$ components is:
  $$m = n - k$$
  Since $k \ge 1$:
  $$m = n - k \le n - 1$$
  However, we are given $m \ge n$.
  This implies $n \le m \le n - 1$, which means $n \le n - 1$, a blatant contradiction.
  Therefore, $G$ must contain at least one cycle. $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 5: Degree Bounds in Bipartite Partitions

**Problem:** Let $G = (R \cup L, E)$ be a bipartite graph where $\forall x \in R, \; \deg(x) \ge d$. Prove that $|L| \ge \frac{|R| \cdot d}{\Delta(L)}$.

### Formal Proof:
1. In any bipartite graph $G = (R \cup L, E)$, every edge has one endpoint in $R$ and one endpoint in $L$.
2. We can compute the total number of edges $|E|$ by summing the degrees from the $R$ partition, or from the $L$ partition:
   $$|E| = \sum_{x \in R} \deg(x) = \sum_{y \in L} \deg(y)$$
3. **Lower bounding $|E|$ via $R$:**
   Since each $x \in R$ satisfies $\deg(x) \ge d$:
   $$|E| = \sum_{x \in R} \deg(x) \ge \sum_{x \in R} d = |R| \cdot d$$
4. **Upper bounding $|E|$ via $L$:**
   Since $\Delta(L) = \max_{y \in L} \deg(y)$, every vertex $y \in L$ satisfies $\deg(y) \le \Delta(L)$:
   $$|E| = \sum_{y \in L} \deg(y) \le \sum_{y \in L} \Delta(L) = |L| \cdot \Delta(L)$$
5. **Combining both inequalities:**
   $$|R| \cdot d \le |E| \le |L| \cdot \Delta(L)$$
   $$|R| \cdot d \le |L| \cdot \Delta(L)$$
6. Assuming $d > 0$, $\Delta(L) \ge d > 0$. Dividing both sides by $\Delta(L) > 0$:
   $$|L| \ge \frac{|R| \cdot d}{\Delta(L)}$$
   $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 6: Graph Diameter with Low Maximum Degree

**Problem:** Let $G = (V, E)$ have $n > 1$ vertices with $\forall v, \; \deg(v) < \sqrt{n - 1}$. Prove $\operatorname{diam}(G) \ge 3$.

### Formal Proof:
1. If $G$ is disconnected, its diameter is $\infty \ge 3$, and we are done.
2. Suppose $G$ is connected. Assume for contradiction that:
   $$\operatorname{diam}(G) \le 2$$
3. Fix an arbitrary vertex $u \in V$.
4. Since $\operatorname{diam}(G) \le 2$, the distance from $u$ to any other vertex $v \in V \setminus \{u\}$ is either $1$ or $2$.
   Thus, every vertex in $V \setminus \{u\}$ belongs to:
   - $N_1(u) = \{v \in V \mid \operatorname{dist}(u, v) = 1\}$ (the direct neighbors of $u$).
   - $N_2(u) = \{v \in V \mid \operatorname{dist}(u, v) = 2\}$ (vertices at distance 2).
5. The total number of vertices in $G$ is:
   $$n = 1 + |N_1(u)| + |N_2(u)|$$
6. Let $\Delta = \max_{v \in V} \deg(v)$. We are given that $\Delta < \sqrt{n - 1}$.
   - The number of direct neighbors is $|N_1(u)| = \deg(u) \le \Delta$.
   - Every vertex in $N_2(u)$ must be adjacent to at least one vertex in $N_1(u)$.
   - Each neighbor $w \in N_1(u)$ has at most $\deg(w) - 1 \le \Delta - 1$ neighbors outside of $\{u\}$.
   - Summing over all $w \in N_1(u)$:
     $$|N_2(u)| \le \sum_{w \in N_1(u)} (\deg(w) - 1) \le |N_1(u)| \cdot (\Delta - 1) \le \Delta(\Delta - 1)$$
7. Substituting these bounds into the vertex count:
   $$n \le 1 + \Delta + \Delta(\Delta - 1) = 1 + \Delta + \Delta^2 - \Delta = 1 + \Delta^2$$
8. Since $\Delta < \sqrt{n - 1}$, squaring both sides yields:
   $$\Delta^2 < n - 1 \implies 1 + \Delta^2 < n$$
9. Combining the two inequalities:
   $$n \le 1 + \Delta^2 < n \implies n < n$$
   This is a contradiction.
   Therefore, our assumption that $\operatorname{diam}(G) \le 2$ must be false, so:
   $$\operatorname{diam}(G) \ge 3$$
   $\blacksquare$

## 📌 Solution 7: Non-Existence of Bridges in $k$-Regular Bipartite Graphs

**Problem:** Prove that for $k \ge 2$, no $k$-regular bipartite graph contains a bridge.

### Formal Proof:
1. Let $G = (A \cup B, E)$ be a $k$-regular bipartite graph with $k \ge 2$.
2. Assume for contradiction that there exists an edge $e = (u, v) \in E$ that is a **bridge**.
3. Since $G$ is bipartite, every edge connects a vertex in $A$ with a vertex in $B$. Without loss of generality, let $u \in A$ and $v \in B$.
4. Removing $e$ disconnects the component containing $e$ into exactly two separate connected components $G_1$ and $G_2$.
   Let $u \in V(G_1)$ and $v \in V(G_2)$.
5. Partition the vertices of $G_1$ according to the original bipartition:
   $$A_1 = A \cap V(G_1) \quad \text{and} \quad B_1 = B \cap V(G_1)$$
6. Since $e = (u, v)$ was the **only** edge connecting $G_1$ to the rest of the graph:
   - For every vertex $x \in A_1 \setminus \{u\}$, all of its $k$ original edges in $G$ remain intact inside $G_1$. Thus $\deg_{G_1}(x) = k$.
   - For vertex $u \in A_1$, the edge $e = (u, v)$ was removed, leaving $\deg_{G_1}(u) = k - 1$.
   - For every vertex $y \in B_1$, since $v \notin B_1$ (as $v \in V(G_2)$), none of the vertices in $B_1$ were incident to $e$. Thus all their $k$ edges remain in $G_1$: $\deg_{G_1}(y) = k$.
7. Now, compute the total number of edges $|E(G_1)|$ by counting degrees from $A_1$ and from $B_1$:
   - From side $A_1$:
     $$|E(G_1)| = \sum_{x \in A_1} \deg_{G_1}(x) = (\deg_{G_1}(u)) + \sum_{x \in A_1 \setminus \{u\}} \deg_{G_1}(x) = (k - 1) + (|A_1| - 1)k = k|A_1| - 1$$
   - From side $B_1$:
     $$|E(G_1)| = \sum_{y \in B_1} \deg_{G_1}(y) = \sum_{y \in B_1} k = k|B_1|$$
8. Equating the two expressions for $|E(G_1)|$:
   $$k|B_1| = k|A_1| - 1$$
   Rearranging gives:
   $$k(|A_1| - |B_1|) = 1$$
9. Note that $k$ and $(|A_1| - |B_1|)$ are integers.
   Since $k \ge 2$, $k$ cannot divide $1$ (the only integer divisors of $1$ are $\pm 1$).
   This is an impossible contradiction!
10. Therefore, the bridge $e$ cannot exist. Every $k$-regular bipartite graph with $k \ge 2$ contains no bridges. $\blacksquare$
